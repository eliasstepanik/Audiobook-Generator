The Signal

The observatory was silent, save for the low hum of machines that had been running for decades without purpose. Dr. Elena Vasquez sat alone in the control room, her coffee long since gone cold, her eyes fixed on the same empty frequency readout she had stared at for three years.

"Nothing again," she muttered to herself, rubbing her tired eyes. "Just like yesterday. Just like always."

She reached for the power switch. There was no point anymore. The funding was gone. Tomorrow, they would begin dismantling the array.

Then the screen flickered.

"What the—" Elena froze, her hand hovering above the switch. A pattern emerged from the noise. Not random. Not interference. Something deliberate.

"Oh my God," she whispered, barely breathing. "Oh my God, it's real."

Her fingers trembled as she reached for the phone. She dialed Commander James Torres at mission control. It rang once. Twice. Three times.

"Torres here. It's two in the morning, Vasquez. This better be—"

"Shut up and listen," Elena cut him off, her voice shaking. "I'm receiving a signal. A structured, repeating signal from Proxima Centauri. James, this is not a drill."

A long silence stretched across the line.

"I'm on my way," Torres said quietly, all traces of sleep gone from his voice. "Don't touch anything. Don't tell anyone. I'll be there in twenty minutes."

Elena turned back to the screen. The pattern pulsed steadily, like a heartbeat from across the stars. She pressed the translation algorithm and waited.

The computer processed for what felt like an eternity. Then, line by line, words began to appear on the screen.

WE HAVE BEEN LISTENING. WE KNOW YOU ARE THERE. DO NOT BE AFRAID.

Elena stared at the words until they blurred. Her whole body was shaking now.

"They know we're here," she said to the empty room, tears streaming down her face. "They've been listening the whole time."

Twenty minutes later, Torres burst through the door, rain dripping from his coat.

"Show me," he demanded, breathing hard from running.

Elena simply pointed at the screen. Torres read the message once. Then again. Then a third time.

"This changes everything," he said, his voice barely above a whisper. "Every single thing we thought we knew."

He pulled out his secure phone and dialed. When the line connected, he spoke with the weight of a man who understood that the world would never be the same.

"Mr. President, I apologize for the hour. But I need you to listen very carefully. We are not alone."

The signal continued to pulse through the long, dark night, steady and patient, as if it had been waiting for them all along.
